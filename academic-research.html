Both cases below follow the same structure: the task as specified, an assumption that did not survive testing, and what was validated before anything was reported.

Lead Case · With Hexi Zhao · Advisor Adam Ousherovitch · Department of Statistics

Conditional Pass@N and Search-Aligned Training

Standard LLM training optimizes single-sample likelihood, which creates a mismatch with test-time strategies that generate multiple candidates and aggregate them. This project investigated whether conditional sampling closes that gap, and under what conditions.

Task as specified

Fine-tune GPT-2 on 1,000 problems from the MATH benchmark and compare three test-time sampling strategies — a normal baseline, simple conditional prompting, and a two-stage chunked approach — against a fixed sample budget on 100 held-out problems.

Assumption flagged

An early formulation treated a simplified, greedy approximation of conditional sampling as sufficient to establish the dominance guarantee expected of the full operator. Prompt-based conditioning also ran into a practical limit: instructing the model not to repeat a prior wrong answer could paradoxically raise the odds of that same answer (the "Pink Elephant Effect"), and GPT-2's limited instruction-following made conditioning unreliable, particularly in a chain-of-thought context. Results were suggestive — conditional strategies improved pass@10 by up to fivefold over baseline — but the held-out set was too small for the claim to stand on its own.

Validated before reporting

The greedy-approximation claim was excluded rather than reported, and the underlying question was isolated from prompt-following noise with a second, exact evaluator: a Sampling-Without-Replacement operator over a synthetic categorical distribution (one correct answer at fixed probability, eight rival answers under variable concentration), tested across 1,600 settings (4 budgets × 4 probabilities × 5 concentration levels × 400 trials). Under that exact evaluator, conditional pass@N strictly dominated independent pass@N with zero violations.

Maizey Automated Grading Tool

Students in the PharmD Evidence-Based Medicine course complete seven open-ended appraisal assignments per term, each requiring roughly five hours of graduate-instructor review. This project built and evaluated a retrieval-augmented grading tool inside Maizey, the University of Michigan's institutionally protected AI platform, to reduce that load without giving up grading quality.

Task as specified

Build a grading tool that retrieves course-specific answer keys and evaluates student responses at temperature 0 for reproducible output, then check its classifications against human grading using interrater agreement (Cohen's κ) and confusion-matrix metrics, across three evaluation rounds and all seven assignment types.

Assumption flagged

The first version assumed one general grading prompt would generalize across all seven assignment types, and graded primarily for overlap with the answer key regardless of question type. Round 1 showed that assumption failing directly: 58.6% observed agreement with human graders and κ = 0.22 (fair agreement) — not usable for deployment. The tool was also overly stringent, requiring exact detail-level matches even on judgment-based questions where the core reasoning mattered more than exact wording.

Validated before reporting

Each round's confusion matrix was checked against human grading before any prompt change was kept, and item-level instructions were added specifically where an item produced more than one false positive or more than three false negatives. By round 3, observed agreement reached 91.2% (κ = 0.81, almost perfect agreement) while recall on incorrect responses stayed at 93.0% — prioritized deliberately, since a missed error costs more in a grading context than an over-flagged one. Precision improved from 52.3% to 82.4% across rounds, and the tool's remaining tendency to flag correct answers as incorrect is reported as an open limitation, not smoothed over.
